# Appendix E — Causal Inference

## E.1 Introduction

Every previous appendix treated $P(Y\mid X=x)$ — what we'd *predict* about $Y$ upon *observing* $X=x$. This appendix is about a different question: what would $Y$ do if we *set* $X$ to $x$, reaching in and forcing it, regardless of whatever would ordinarily have caused $X$ to take that value? These are not the same question, and confusing them is the single most common statistical error there is. This appendix builds the formal machinery — structural causal models, the $do$-operator, the back-door and front-door criteria, and the potential-outcomes framework — needed to tell them apart and, when possible, to compute one from data that only gives you the other.

If you can answer these questions, you're ready:

- Why does $P(Y\mid X=x)$ generally differ from $P(Y\mid do(X=x))$, and under exactly what condition do they coincide?
- What does "controlling for confounders" formally license, and why does controlling for the *wrong* variable (e.g. a collider) actively introduce bias rather than removing it?
- Why can the front-door criterion identify a causal effect even when *no* back-door adjustment set is observable?
- What assumption connects the potential-outcomes framework to the $do$-operator, and why is it needed at all?

### E.1.1 Conventions

A **structural causal model (SCM)** is a triple $M = \langle V, U, F\rangle$: endogenous variables $V = \{X_1,\ldots,X_n\}$, mutually independent exogenous noise variables $U = \{U_1,\ldots,U_n\}$, and structural functions $F = \{f_1,\ldots,f_n\}$ assigning each $X_i$ its value. Each $X_i$'s **parents** $\mathrm{Pa}_i \subseteq V\setminus\{X_i\}$ are the endogenous variables that appear in $f_i$. The parent relation induces a directed graph $G$ on $V$; we assume throughout that $G$ is acyclic (a DAG — see Appendix A on graph theory), which is what makes "the value of $X_i$" well-defined at all: with no cycles, the structural equations can be solved in topological order, each $X_i$ computed only from quantities already fixed.

Because the $U_i$ are mutually independent, the observational joint distribution **factorizes according to $G$**:

$$
P(v) = \prod_i P(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i)
$$

which is the same Markov factorization that defines a Bayesian network (Appendix B §B.4) — an SCM is a Bayesian network with an added causal reading of its edges, and everything below is about exploiting that extra reading.

## E.2 Structural Equations and Causal Graphs

### E.2.1 Structural Equations

Each endogenous variable is generated by

$$
X_i = f_i(\mathrm{Pa}_i, U_i)
$$

This is stronger than a regression equation: $f_i$ is not merely a good predictor of $X_i$ from $\mathrm{Pa}_i$, it is asserted to be the actual data-generating mechanism, invariant to what happens elsewhere in the model. That invariance is the entire content of "causal" in causal model — it is exactly the assumption that licenses asking what happens if we *change* one equation while leaving all the others alone (§E.3).

### E.2.2 Why the Graph Alone Isn't Enough

Two SCMs can induce the identical DAG $G$ and identical observational distribution $P(v)$ while disagreeing about every interventional question — because $P(v)$ only fixes the conditional distributions $P(x_i\mid\mathrm{pa}_i)$, not the specific mechanism $f_i$ that produced them, and different mechanisms can respond differently to being overwritten. This is why causal inference cannot, even in principle, be reduced to finding patterns in observational data alone (§B.4's Bayes-net machinery): the DAG plus $P(v)$ determines every observational and interventional query only once we also assume the *edges represent mechanisms*, not just statistical dependence — an assumption that has to come from outside the data (domain knowledge, an experiment, or a temporal/physical argument such as "cause precedes effect").

## E.3 Interventions and the $do$-Operator

### E.3.1 $do(X=x)$ vs. Conditioning on $X=x$

**Conditioning** ($X=x$ observed): restrict attention to the subpopulation where $X$ happened to equal $x$. Every other variable's distribution updates through *all* its statistical dependencies with $X$ — including dependencies that run backward, through $X$'s causes.

**Intervention** ($do(X=x)$): replace the structural equation $f_X(\mathrm{Pa}_X, U_X)$ with the constant function $X := x$, deleting every incoming edge to $X$ in the graph. This produces a **mutilated graph** $G_{\overline{X}}$ and a **mutilated SCM** $M_x$ in which $X$ no longer responds to its former causes at all — it is fixed by fiat.

This is why $P(Y\mid X=x) \ne P(Y\mid do(X=x))$ in general: conditioning leaves $X$'s incoming edges intact, so information can still flow backward from $X$ to its parents and onward to $Y$ through any variable $Y$ shares with those parents; intervention severs exactly that backward flow, so only the *forward*, genuinely causal, pathways from $X$ to $Y$ remain.

### E.3.2 The Truncated Factorization

Applying the ordinary Markov factorization (§E.1.1) to the mutilated SCM $M_x$ gives the **truncated factorization** (also called the manipulated distribution or the $g$-formula):

$$
P(v \mid do(x)) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle\prod_{i \,:\, X_i \ne X} P(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i) & \text{if } v \text{ is consistent with } X=x \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
$$

— the same product as the observational factorization, but with the factor $P(x\mid\mathrm{pa}_X)$ simply dropped, since $X$ no longer depends on $\mathrm{pa}_X$ at all. This single formula is the foundation everything else in this appendix reduces to; the back-door and front-door criteria (§E.4–E.5) are both just recipes for computing marginals of this truncated factorization using *only* quantities estimable from observational data, without ever needing to run an actual intervention.

**Minimal worked example.** Let $Z \to X$, $Z \to Y$, $X \to Y$ (a single confounder $Z$ of $X$ and $Y$). By the ordinary factorization, $P(y\mid x) = \sum_z P(y\mid x,z)\,P(z\mid x)$. By the truncated factorization, dropping the (now absent) dependence of $X$ on $Z$:

$$
P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y\mid x,z)\, P(z)
$$

The two expressions differ exactly in whether $z$ is weighted by $P(z\mid x)$ or by $P(z)$ — they coincide only when $Z \perp X$, i.e. only when there is no confounding to begin with. This is the precise, general sense in which "$P(Y\mid X)$ conflates correlation with causation": it reweights the confounder by the *wrong* distribution.

> **MI connection**: This is exactly the distinction between *observing* an activation take some value across a dataset (conditioning) and *setting* it via activation patching / ablation, then measuring downstream behavior (intervening). A neuron that merely correlates with a behavior because both share an upstream cause (e.g. both are downstream of the same earlier feature) will show up in $P(\text{behavior}\mid \text{activation})$ but vanish under $do(\text{activation})$ patching — which is precisely why activation patching, not correlational probing, is treated as the standard for a causal claim in interpretability.

## E.4 Confounding and the Back-Door Criterion

### E.4.1 Definition

A set of variables $Z$ satisfies the **back-door criterion** relative to an ordered pair $(X,Y)$ if:

1. No node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and
2. $Z$ blocks every path between $X$ and $Y$ that contains an arrow *into* $X$ (a "back-door path" — any path leaving $X$ backward, i.e. through one of its causes, rather than forward through its effects).

Condition (1) rules out controlling for a *consequence* of the treatment (which can introduce bias even when it removes none, e.g. conditioning on a collider); condition (2) is what makes $Z$ sufficient to screen off confounding without needing to screen off the genuine causal pathways forward from $X$, which never contain an arrow into $X$ and so are never blocked by $Z$.

### E.4.2 Back-Door Adjustment Formula

If $Z$ satisfies the back-door criterion for $(X,Y)$, the causal effect is identifiable from purely observational quantities by

$$
P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z)
$$

**Why this is exactly right, not just plausible**: condition (2) guarantees $Z$ $d$-separates $X$ from $Y$ in the mutilated graph $G_{\overline{X}}$ along every remaining path once the back-door paths are blocked and the forward paths are unaffected by the mutilation (they don't touch $X$'s incoming edges), so $P(y\mid do(x), z) = P(y\mid x, z)$ — intervening and conditioning agree *once $Z$ is fixed*. Averaging that identity over the (interventionally unaffected, since $Z$ is not a descendant of $X$) marginal $P(z) = P(z\mid do(x))$ gives the formula.

### E.4.3 Worked Example

Continue the confounder example of §E.3.2 with concrete numbers. Suppose $Z\in\{0,1\}$ with $P(Z=1)=0.5$, and

$$
P(X=1\mid Z=0) = 0.2, \qquad P(X=1\mid Z=1) = 0.8
$$

$$
P(Y=1\mid X=0,Z=0)=0.1,\ \ P(Y=1\mid X=1,Z=0)=0.5,\ \ P(Y=1\mid X=0,Z=1)=0.3,\ \ P(Y=1\mid X=1,Z=1)=0.7
$$

The naive conditional effect uses $P(Z\mid X=1) \propto P(X=1\mid Z)P(Z)$, giving $P(Z=1\mid X=1) = 0.8/(0.2+0.8)=0.8$, so

$$
P(Y=1\mid X=1) = 0.2\cdot P(Y{=}1\mid X{=}1,Z{=}0) + 0.8\cdot P(Y{=}1\mid X{=}1,Z{=}1) = 0.2(0.5)+0.8(0.7)=0.66
$$

The back-door-adjusted causal effect instead weights $Z$ by its *marginal* $P(z)=0.5$ regardless of $x$:

$$
P(Y=1\mid do(X=1)) = 0.5(0.5) + 0.5(0.7) = 0.60
$$

The gap, $0.66$ vs. $0.60$, is entirely attributable to $Z$ being over-represented at its high-$Y$ value ($Z=1$) among the $X=1$ subpopulation — exactly the confounding bias the back-door formula removes.

## E.5 The Front-Door Criterion

### E.5.1 Motivation and Definition

The back-door criterion requires an *observed* set $Z$ that blocks all confounding — but sometimes the confounder $U$ between $X$ and $Y$ is unobserved, and no such $Z$ exists. The front-door criterion handles a specific such case: suppose there is a mediator $M$ that carries *all* of $X$'s effect on $Y$ (i.e. $X \to M \to Y$ is the only forward path), such that:

1. $M$ intercepts every directed path from $X$ to $Y$,
2. There is no unblocked back-door path from $X$ to $M$ (no confounder shared by $X$ and $M$), and
3. Every back-door path from $M$ to $Y$ is blocked by $X$ (i.e. conditioning on $X$ screens off any confounding between $M$ and $Y$).

Then $\{M\}$ is said to satisfy the **front-door criterion** relative to $(X,Y)$, and the causal effect of $X$ on $Y$ is identifiable *even though $U$ is never observed*:

$$
P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid x', m)\, P(x')
$$

### E.5.2 Why This Works: Two Back-Door Applications Chained

The formula is two applications of §E.4.2 composed:

- **Step 1** ($X \to M$): condition (2) says $\varnothing$ already satisfies the back-door criterion for $(X, M)$ — there's nothing to adjust for — so $P(m \mid do(x)) = P(m\mid x)$ directly.
- **Step 2** ($M \to Y$): condition (3) says $X$ itself satisfies the back-door criterion for $(M, Y)$, so $P(y\mid do(m)) = \sum_{x'} P(y\mid m, x')\,P(x')$.

Chaining these — the effect of $do(x)$ on $M$ from Step 1, then the effect of that $M$-distribution on $Y$ via Step 2 — gives exactly the formula above. The classic illustration is smoking ($X$) $\to$ tar deposits ($M$) $\to$ cancer ($Y$), with an unobserved genotype $U$ suspected to confound smoking and cancer directly; if genotype affects cancer risk only *through* smoking-caused tar (not tar deposited any other way) and doesn't itself affect tar deposition given smoking status, the front door through $M=\text{tar}$ identifies the causal effect of smoking on cancer without ever measuring genotype.

> **MI connection**: Front-door-style reasoning is the logical structure behind **path patching**: rather than asking only "does patching this upstream component change the output" (a back-door-style total-effect question), path patching isolates the effect that flows through one specific downstream path (e.g. one attention head feeding into one later head) while holding the direct path fixed — mirroring how the front-door formula isolates the $X\to M\to Y$ channel from any direct or confounded $X\to Y$ influence. Mediation analysis in interpretability (decomposing a total causal effect into direct and indirect components through specific circuit paths) is precisely the front-door decomposition applied to a computation graph instead of an SCM over "natural" variables.

## E.6 The Potential-Outcomes Framework

### E.6.1 Definitions

For a binary treatment $X\in\{0,1\}$, each unit $i$ is posited to have two **potential outcomes**: $Y_i(1)$, what $Y$ would be for unit $i$ under treatment, and $Y_i(0)$, what it would be under control. The **individual treatment effect** is $\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$, and the **average treatment effect** is

$$
\mathrm{ATE} = E[Y(1) - Y(0)]
$$

The **fundamental problem of causal inference** is that for any given unit, only one of $Y_i(1), Y_i(0)$ is ever observed — whichever corresponds to the treatment that unit actually received — so $\tau_i$ is never directly measurable for any individual; all of causal inference from this framework is about what can nonetheless be recovered about *aggregates* like the ATE.

### E.6.2 Consistency and SUTVA

The link between potential outcomes and the single observed outcome is the **consistency** assumption:

$$
Y_i = X_i\, Y_i(1) + (1-X_i)\, Y_i(0)
$$

i.e. the potential outcome under the treatment actually received is exactly the observed outcome — not merely close to it. This is bundled with the **Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA)**: one unit's potential outcomes don't depend on another unit's treatment assignment (no interference/spillover), and there is only a single version of "treatment" (no hidden variation in what "treated" means across units). Both are substantive, checkable-in-principle assumptions, not free consequences of the notation — violating SUTVA (e.g. treating one unit affects a neighboring unit's outcome, as in vaccination or social-network settings) breaks every identification result below.

### E.6.3 Equivalence to the $do$-Operator, and Identification

Within an SCM, define $Y_x(u) := Y$ as computed when $X$'s structural equation is replaced by the constant $x$ but every $U_i = u_i$ is held at its actual realized value — this *is* the formal definition of the potential outcome $Y(x)$, so the potential-outcomes framework and the $do$-calculus framework are two notations for the same underlying object, provable to agree wherever both are well-defined.

Potential outcomes are identifiable from observational data under an **ignorability** (unconfoundedness) assumption,

$$
(Y(0), Y(1)) \perp X \mid Z
$$

for some observed covariate set $Z$ — which is exactly the back-door criterion of §E.4.1 restated in potential-outcomes notation: $Z$ blocks all the "backward" statistical dependence between treatment assignment and the outcome the unit *would have had*. Under ignorability, the ATE is identified by an averaged version of the back-door formula:

$$
\mathrm{ATE} = E_Z\big[\, E[Y\mid X=1, Z] - E[Y\mid X=0, Z]\,\big]
$$

which reduces, when $Z$ is empty (randomized treatment, the strongest form of ignorability), to the naive difference in means $E[Y\mid X=1] - E[Y\mid X=0]$ — the formal reason randomized experiments don't need covariate adjustment: randomization is what makes $Z=\varnothing$ already satisfy the back-door criterion, since it severs every incoming edge to $X$ by design rather than by post-hoc adjustment.

## E.7 Common Identities Reference

**Structural causal model**

$$
X_i = f_i(\mathrm{Pa}_i, U_i), \qquad P(v) = \prod_i P(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i)
$$

**Truncated factorization (the $do$-operator)**

$$
P(v \mid do(x)) = \prod_{i\,:\,X_i \ne X} P(x_i \mid \mathrm{pa}_i) \quad \text{(restricted to } v \text{ consistent with } X=x\text{)}
$$

**Back-door adjustment**

$$
P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\, P(z)
$$

**Front-door adjustment**

$$
P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_m P(m \mid x) \sum_{x'} P(y \mid x', m)\, P(x')
$$

**Potential outcomes**

$$
Y_i = X_i\, Y_i(1) + (1-X_i)\, Y_i(0), \qquad \mathrm{ATE} = E[Y(1)-Y(0)]
$$

**ATE under ignorability**

$$
\mathrm{ATE} = E_Z\big[\, E[Y\mid X=1,Z] - E[Y\mid X=0,Z]\,\big]
$$

## E.8 Summary: MI-Relevant Causal Inference Concepts

| Concept | MI Application |
|---|---|
| $do(X=x)$ vs. conditioning | The formal distinction between correlational probing and activation patching / ablation |
| Truncated factorization | Justifies why patching one activation shouldn't be expected to change the *upstream* computation that fed it |
| Back-door criterion | Choosing a valid corrupted/reference distribution in causal scrubbing so the comparison isolates the component of interest |
| Colliders and improper conditioning | Why conditioning on a downstream (post-treatment) activation can introduce spurious dependence rather than remove it |
| Front-door / mediation | The logical structure behind path patching and direct-vs-indirect effect decomposition across circuit paths |
| Potential outcomes / ATE | The average causal effect of ablating a feature or circuit component, aggregated across many prompts |
| Ignorability / SUTVA | The implicit assumptions behind treating patched-prompt results as generalizing (no interference between examples, a well-defined single "intervention") |
| Fundamental problem of causal inference | Why a single forward pass can't reveal a component's causal role — only comparison across interventions can |
